Biological Sex & the Lifespan Strength Arc

Normative life-course studies establish that human grip strength rises through adolescence, peaks in early-to-mid adulthood, and declines steadily in later life, with substantial separation between men and women (Dodds et al., 2014). Reproducing this trajectory in our 10,987 NHANES examinees (ages 16–80+) demonstrates why sex dimorphism and nonlinear aging must be modeled before interpreting any sleep association.

Chapter 2 Analytical Takeaway

Combined grip strength peaks in ages 30–44 (97.4 kg Male, 61.3 kg Female survey-weighted means) before declining 32.8% in men and 35.5% in women by ages 75–80+—an age swing over ten times larger than typical sleep-tier differences.

Why linear age adjustment fails: Because male combined grip rises from ~83 kg at ages 16–19 to ~98 kg at 30–34 before falling to ~64 kg at 80+, fitting a straight line for age misrepresents both adolescents/young adults and older adults. Both ends of that nonlinear curve (16–19 and 75–80+) also contain higher proportions of 9+h sleepers—which is why flexible nonlinear age modeling (ns(age, df = 5)) was used in Chapter 1.

Distinguishing descriptive subgroup differences from covariate adjustment and survey weighting: The chart and table above document descriptive differences across NHANES RIDRETH3 categories—for example, Non-Hispanic Black examinees represent 24.0% of the unweighted sample (oversampled relative to their 11.6% MEC-weighted U.S. population share; CDC/NCHS Analytic Guidelines, 2018) and exhibit both higher mean grip strength and higher weighted &le;6h sleep prevalence (48.9% vs. 31.9% in Non-Hispanic White examinees). Descriptive differences alone do not prove statistical confounding, so we verified this directly via regression comparison: in unweighted sex * ns(age, df = 5) OLS, adding RIDRETH3 shifts the &le;5h vs. 7–8h coefficient from +1.03 kg to -0.06 kg (p = 0.88). Applying MEC survey weights (WTMEC2YR / 2) separately recalibrates the sample to target U.S. population proportions (+0.30 kg, p = 0.60 in Chapter 1’s Model 4), without implying any biological or causal mechanism behind subgroup differences.
