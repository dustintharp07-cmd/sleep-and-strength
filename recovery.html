Does Habitual Short Sleep Erode Maximal Grip Strength?

Prior experimental reviews show that acute sleep loss (&le;6h in 24 hours) measurably reduces next-day exercise performance, particularly across prolonged, skill-intensive, and multi-set resistance sessions (Craven et al., 2022; Knowles et al., 2018). Motivated by that literature, we tested whether habitual short weekday sleep (&le;5h or 6h) aligns with weaker isometric grip force in 5,551 younger NHANES examinees (adolescents and prime-age adults, ages 16–44) and across all 10,987 participants (ages 16–80+).

Chapter 1 Analytical Takeaway

Habitual short sleep (&le;5h and 6h) shows no cross-sectional strength deficit relative to 7–8h (+0.17 kg for 6h, p = 0.73 in survey-weighted models), while the observed raw 9+h strength difference is attenuated by approximately 73% after accounting for sex and nonlinear age differences.

Distinguishing background exercise literature from this NHANES result: Systematic reviews of experimental sleep restriction find more consistent decrements in sustained, skill-demanding, or high-volume multi-joint resistance sessions than in brief, isolated maximal isometric tests (Knowles et al., 2018; Craven et al., 2022). Moreover, whereas laboratory trials induce acute sleep loss, NHANES captures habitual self-reported weekday sleep during a brief, 3-trial per hand isometric Takei dynamometer test (CDC/NCHS MGX Manual, 2013). In our NHANES 16–44 analysis, 6h sleepers match 7–8h peers (+0.23 kg, p = 0.70). Notice also that men aged 30–44 exceed ages 16–29 across sleep tiers because adolescent males (16–21) are still gaining muscle mass.

Interpreting statistical attenuation across the four models: In unadjusted sample data (Model 1), &le;5h and 6h sleepers average +1.28 kg and +1.55 kg relative to 7–8h sleepers, while 9+h sleepers average -5.27 kg. Model 2 (+ sex) accounts for sex differences in average strength, and Model 3 (+ sex * ns(age, df = 5)) accounts for nonlinear age differences across 16–80+, attenuating the observed 9+h difference by approximately 73% to -1.43 kg. Finally, Model 4 incorporates NHANES 4-year MEC weights (WTMEC2YR / 2), strata (SDMVSTRA), and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), yielding null population mean differences for short sleep (&le;5h: +0.30 kg, p = 0.60; 6h: +0.17 kg, p = 0.73) and a modest -1.84 kg (p = 0.0067) association for 9+h. Note that covariate attenuation in cross-sectional models summarizes statistical adjustment and does not establish a causal mechanism.
