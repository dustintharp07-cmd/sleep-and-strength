Across the Lifespan: Sleep Quality & Functional Aging

Clinical research establishes handgrip dynamometry as an indicator of overall muscle strength and a predictor of physical disability and health trajectories, particularly in middle and older adulthood (Bohannon, 2019; Dodds et al., 2014). Building on that clinical context, we test where across all five NHANES age groups (16–80+) long sleep (9+h) diverges from 7–8h, evaluate subjective sleep complaints (SLQ050) and diagnosed sleep disorders (SLQ060), and check household socioeconomic covariate sensitivity (INDFMPIR).

Chapter 3 Analytical Takeaway

Across ages 16–59, neither short (&le;5h) nor long (9+h) sleep differs significantly from 7–8h in survey-weighted models; the 9+h strength deficit appears specifically in ages 60–74 (-3.97 kg) and 75–80+ (-3.18 kg), while reported sleep trouble and diagnosed sleep disorders show null adjusted associations overall.

Long Sleep (9+h) Shows Negligible Strength Differences From Ages 16 Through 59, Diverging Significantly Only After Age 60

Survey-weighted adjusted differences (kg, ±95% CI) comparing Very Short (≤5h) and Long (9+h) sleepers against Recommended (7–8h) peers within each life stage.

Observational interpretation vs. prior epidemiological literature: Because NHANES is cross-sectional, the lower grip strength observed here among older 9+h sleepers (-3.97 kg in 60–74; -3.18 kg in 75–80+) does not show that sleeping 9+ hours weakens muscle. Prior epidemiological investigations demonstrate that habitual long sleep (&ge;9h) frequently co-occurs with chronic medical conditions, depressive symptoms, sedative/antidepressant medication use, or recovery from illness (Patel et al., 2006; Watson et al., 2015)—factors not controlled in these models that can independently reduce physical strength and increase time spent sleeping.

NHANES Survey-Weighted Means (95% CI) SLQ050 Analytical Sample: n = 10,986 of 10,987 (1 Missing Excluded)

Self-Reported Sleep Trouble (SLQ050) and Diagnosed Sleep Disorders (SLQ060) Show No Meaningful Independent Association With Grip Strength Once Age and Sex Are Controlled

Survey-weighted mean grip strength (±95% CI) across all five life stages for examinees who reported telling a doctor they had trouble sleeping (Yes vs. No). Toggle to inspect physician-diagnosed sleep disorders (SLQ060).

Reporting null findings transparently: Across all 10,986 examinees with valid SLQ050 responses (1 refused/don’t know), reporting sleep trouble is associated with just -0.83 kg (95% CI [-1.86, +0.20], p = 0.11) after accounting for sex, nonlinear age, and NHANES survey design. Similarly, across 10,973 valid SLQ060 responses (14 missing), a diagnosed sleep disorder shows -1.21 kg (95% CI [-2.88, +0.46], p = 0.15). Unadjusted differences in pooled summaries arise largely because sleep complaints become more common in middle and older age.

NHANES Survey-Weighted Means (95% CI) INDFMPIR Complete-Case Slice: n = 10,119 of 10,987 (868 [7.9%] Missing Excluded)

Higher Household Income-to-Poverty Ratio Aligns With Modestly Stronger Grip (+0.75 kg per PIR Unit), Without Explaining Away the 9+h Association

Combined grip strength (±95% CI) across four CDC family poverty-income ratio (INDFMPIR) tiers separated by sex. Participants missing household income (n = 868) are explicitly excluded rather than imputed.

Socioeconomic sensitivity check: Adding continuous INDFMPIR (n = 10,119) to our survey-weighted sex * ns(age, df = 5) regression confirms a positive socioeconomic gradient (+0.75 kg per PIR unit, 95% CI [+0.50, +1.00], p < 0.0001), while short sleep remains null (&le;5h: +0.50 kg, p = 0.37; 6h: +0.26 kg, p = 0.63) and Long (9+h) remains -1.63 kg (95% CI [-2.88, -0.39], p = 0.0131). Unmerged NHANES modules—such as body anthropometric measures (BMX) and physical activity (PAQ)—remain outside this three-module dataset and represent important covariates for future work.
